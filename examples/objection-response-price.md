# Example, written response to the price objection

Audience: finance director, after a security call. Channel: email follow-up. Grounded in
`../reference/objection-handling.md`. Approved 2026-09-04.

---

## The objection, verbatim from the room

"Microsoft gives us Copilot for less than half that, and it is already in the agreement we signed."

---

## The asset

Nasser, you are right, and I would rather say so plainly than work around it.

Per seat, Copilot is cheaper than our landed cost, and it is already inside a contract you have
negotiated and your procurement team has approved. If the question in front of you is the cost of
giving people an assistant, that is the better answer and I will not pretend otherwise.

The question we are answering is a different one. An assistant drafts and summarises inside
Microsoft's own boundary. What we deploy executes: it reads and writes files on the employee's
machine, runs commands under per-role scopes, and completes multi-step work without a person in
each step. That capability is worth more and it also creates an evidence problem, which is why the
approval gates, the per-user quota and the action-level audit log in your own store exist.

Three things follow from that, and they are the whole of my argument.

Inference runs on your account, so your organisational data does not reach us at all. That is not
a residency commitment we would ask your team to defend to the regulator, it is the absence of a
path, and your team can trace it on the diagram we walked through on Tuesday.

The bill is one you set. SAR 79 per seat is fixed, the quota is a hard cap rather than an alert,
and the SAR 45 to 55 of inference is billed to you by your own provider with no margin to us. We
have no reason to want your token count to rise.

And the work is deployed into your systems, including the internal ones, by the people who built
the harness. That is the line item you are actually buying at SAR 60,000, and it is what makes the
difference between a licence and something in production in six to eight weeks.

If governance and execution are not live questions for you this year, buy the cheaper product.
That is a genuine recommendation and it will save us both a quarter.

---

## Why this works

**It concedes the true part first and completely.** The competitor is cheaper per seat. Any answer
that opens by disputing an accurate number spends the credibility needed for the rest of the
paragraph.

**It moves the comparison rather than winning it.** Assistant against agent is the honest frame.
The response never claims Wolffish is a better assistant, which would be a losing and unprovable
claim.

**It names the cost of the capability it is selling.** Saying that execution creates an evidence
problem, then naming the controls that answer it, is more persuasive than selling the controls
alone.

**All three price lines appear, and the pass-through is marked as customer-billed with no margin.**
The sentence about having no reason to want the token count to rise is the mechanism behind the
claim, not a promise.

**It ends by offering the competitor.** A recommendation to buy elsewhere is the single most
credible sentence available to a two-person company with no reference customer, and it is only
credible because the disqualifier list is published and used.

**No superlative appears anywhere.** No only, first, best or never. No claim about Microsoft's
architecture beyond what its own documentation states, which is the boundary the claim ledger sets
for competitor statements.
