# Capability matrix

Distils the product feature matrix. Load this to decide what leads a message and what is only
claimed. Status flags live in `../rules/feature-status.md`, which is authoritative.

Thirty-six capabilities scored on demand and competitive advantage, each tagged to one of four
layers. **Scores are never averaged across layers**, because a commodity in one layer can be the
reason a deal closes in another.

## The four layers

| Layer | What it is | Who it wins | Commoditisation risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, agentic harness | The runtime on the employee's machine | The employee, then their manager | **High.** Competing harnesses converge every quarter. |
| B, platform control plane | Quotas, audit, kill switch, identity, scopes | The security officer and finance | Medium. Vendors are closing this inside their own clouds. |
| C, cloud service | Deployment, custom integration, ongoing capability work, escrow | Procurement, legal, the owner of the internal system | Low. Does not commoditise, and does not scale. |
| D, Saudi regulatory fit | Entity, invoicing, no cross-border transfer, evidence pack | Procurement, legal, compliance, and the regulator behind them | Lowest. A foreign competitor cannot acquire this in under two years. |

## Lead with these

Highest demand and highest advantage. Nineteen capabilities sit here. The eight that carry a message
on their own:

- No vendor-side data path. The strongest single claim in the product.
- Compliance by architecture rather than by contract, so no transfer mechanism is required.
- First-class integration into the internal systems no vendor catalogue will ever support.
- Saudi entity, riyal contracting, compliant invoicing. Highest raw demand in the matrix, and
almost never articulated as a capability.
- Action-level audit log in the customer's own store.
- The hard enforced quota, as a cap rather than an alert.
- The persistent per-employee agent with real permissions.
- The Arabic-first agent, which is different from Arabic support.

## Claim, never lead

High demand, low advantage. Their absence loses the deal at the security or identity review. Their
presence wins nothing.

Multi-step autonomous execution. Chat and retrieval over company documents. Kill switch. Single
sign-on and offboarding. Admin console. The service level.

**In-Kingdom support hours and Arabic documentation belong here.** Every local integrator already
has them. Treating them as an advantage is the most common mistake a foreign-authored plan makes
about this market.

## Introduce only after the problem is named

Low demand, high advantage. Buyers do not ask for these, and two of them frighten people if they
arrive early.

Shell and command execution, which alarms a security officer if it arrives before approval gates are
explained. Model portability, which nobody asks for and everybody values once vendor lock-in is
named. Source access and escrow, which removes a blocker rather than creating a want. Founder
engineering delivery, which some read as concentration risk until escrow answers it. Conformity for
public-sector data. The sovereign endpoint option, which exists to neutralise one objection.

## Do not build, and say so plainly

Prompt libraries. Usage analytics of message counts, as distinct from spend by team and task. A
mobile application, which is awkward because the agent's value comes from executing on a machine
with files and a shell. "Mobile is not what this product is" beats putting it on a roadmap.

## The uncomfortable reading

Layer A holds three of the four most negative competitive scores. **The harness layer is where
Wolffish is least defensible and is commoditising fastest.** Positioning must not rest there, and
any deck built on harness capability has a twelve-month shelf life.

## The demand driver

One capability generates the top of the funnel: the **Agent Exposure and Spend Audit**, a one-week
founder-led read producing a residency exposure map and a twelve-month cost trajectory.

It works because both findings are structurally unfixable inside a vendor-cloud product. Nobody
resolves cross-border exposure by buying a better tier, because the data path is the product. Nobody
resolves cost trajectory on a metered plan, because the meter is the business model.

The audit does not argue for Wolffish. It describes a shape, and Wolffish is the only thing that
fits it. Its own deliverable then becomes the business case: the residency map is the risk section,
the cost trajectory is the return baseline, the department breakdown is the pilot scope.
