# Objection handling

Synthesized from the buying committee blockers, the competitive gaps and the claim ledger. One
heading per objection in the prospect's own words, then the honest answer. Where the honest answer
is "not yet", it says not yet.

An objection file that treats every objection as a misunderstanding trains agents to argue with
prospects, which loses deals in a market built on relationships.
## "Why not the national champion? They are the national platform."

Different products, and we run on their endpoint if you want their model. They build compute, a
model and a marketplace. We are the layer that puts a model on every employee's machine with your
quotas, approval gates and audit log. You will probably buy both. One sentence, then move on.

## "We already pay for Microsoft. It is nearly free to us."

It is cheaper per seat, and you should not pay more without a reason. The reason is what each is
allowed to do. Theirs drafts inside a document. Ours executes on the employee's machine with file
and command permissions you scope, and writes an audit log into your own store. Their own
reviewers name auditing as the gap.

Say the price point first. A buyer who discovers it themselves stops believing the rest.

## "You are two people. What happens if you disappear?"

Source access and escrow, pre-packaged, in the data room before you ask. Your platform keeps
running. **Escrow is PARTIAL today: the deed is drafted and no agent is appointed**, which is why
this answer names a date rather than a state.

Then ask the same question of the integrator, about the four named people on your account.

## "Our integrator can build this."

They can, and they will build it well, once. Then the second use case is a new statement of work
and the fourth is a new procurement cycle. Ask two questions: who owns it in year three, and what
does the fourth use case cost.

## "We will build it internally on open source."

You can, and it will demonstrate well in a fortnight. Price two things honestly: two engineers of
permanent maintenance, which is close to our modelled cost so this is not a price argument, and
what the stack produces when your regulator asks for attributed audit and enforced quotas, which
is nothing.

## "We need to wait for the Microsoft Saudi region."

That solves where the data sits. It does not remove the vendor from the data path, and it does not
put an agent on the employee's machine. Different problems, and only one of them is the one you
described.

## "What certifications do you hold?"

None today, and none is scheduled. That is a real gap and you should weigh it. What the
architecture provides instead is that we never receive your data, so the questions that matter most
to your auditor are about your own environment rather than ours. The evidence pack maps that to the
controls your regulator applies. Never soften this: a claimed certification loses a security officer
permanently.

## "Can you show us a customer like us?"

No. There is no reference customer yet, and we will not imply one. What we can offer is a paid
pilot on your own systems with named success criteria, escrow, and the architecture in front of
your security team before you spend anything.

## "Our data cannot leave the Kingdom."

Correct, and under the cloud regulations it cannot leave even for caching. That is not a constraint
we accommodate. It is the reason the architecture exists: there is no mechanism by which your data
could leave, so you do not need a transfer basis for it.

## "Send us a proposal."

Before a proposal, thirty minutes with your security team in the room. If the architecture is not a
fit, a proposal wastes both our weeks and we will say so on the call.

## "We have no budget line for this."

The budget statement lands in December and the fiscal year is the calendar year, so we should be in
your fourth-quarter conversation rather than this one. Meanwhile the exposure audit is free and
yours to keep.
